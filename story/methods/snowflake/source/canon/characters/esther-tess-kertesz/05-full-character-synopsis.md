# Esther "Tess" Kertesz: full character synopsis

Status: accepted
Snowflake step: 05

This treatment uses evolving close third in the present tense. It follows
Tess's whole story through the tension between verification and trust, from
sole survival to a shared ending.

## Synopsis

Tess wakes in a hospital after the ferry to adults beginning with the fact that
she survived. They tell her she is safe. They tell her she is very lucky.
Someone uses the word miracle before anyone tells her where the rest of her
family is.

When they finally say that nobody else survived, eight-year-old Tess asks for
each person separately. The adults try to answer once for everyone. She asks
again, one name at a time, until they answer one name at a time.

Her memory of the disaster itself is broken into cold, noise, darkness, and
pieces whose order she cannot recover. The hospital supplies a clean account:
there was an accident, rescuers found her, and extraordinary work saved her
life. Reporters and well-meaning adults repeat the same shape. A terrible thing
happened, but a child survived, which gives the story somewhere hopeful to
end.

Tess does not argue with them. At eight, she wants to know why the people who
belong beside her are not there. The word miracle acquires its bitterness
later, after she has heard it used often enough to understand what it removes.
It makes her survival the point of the story and lets everyone else become the
price paid for it.

Close family friends take her home to Fairbanks and become her guardians. An
aunt and cousins who might have taken her do not. Tess grows up with people who
care for her and with a family that is still gone. She learns early that those
two facts can occupy the same life without resolving each other.

As she gets older, she obtains the official accident report, the rescue
records, and the medical account of her recovery. She reads the documents
until she knows their times, decisions, failed approaches, and gaps. Her
survival has no single miraculous cause. It sits at the end of a long chain of
ordinary conditions, any of which could have broken another way.

The records satisfy one need and leave another alone. They tell Tess how she
survived. They do not tell her why, and she stops expecting the second answer
to exist.

The ferry is the first failed system she studies at length, but it is not the
only reason she becomes interested in how things fail. Weather interests her.
So do coastlines, utilities, ports, human judgment, institutional delay, and
the way several tolerable problems can meet and become one intolerable event.
She attends the University of Alaska Fairbanks and eventually builds a career
in compound and cascading risk.

At the North Pacific Resilience Institute, Tess makes models for governments,
tribal nations, ports, ferry systems, utilities, and relief organizations. Her
work begins where a simple probability stops being useful. She follows what
people will know at each stage of a disaster, which choices will still be
available to them, and how a locally sensible response can make the larger
failure worse.

She keeps a life outside the models. Tess collects field guides and specimens,
catalogs mushrooms and tide-pool organisms, watches weather, and remains bad at
identifying birds by sound. She loves shorelines and open water. When she has
to ride a ferry, she stays on the exterior deck with her coat and boots on and
notes the exits, rafts, crew stations, and weather before the vessel leaves.
She does not sleep below the waterline.

At twenty-eight, still living in Alaska, Tess adopts a seven-year-old cat from
a shelter near Fairbanks. The cat is tiny beneath a long black-and-brown coat.
Frostbite has taken the tips of both ears, leaving them rounded enough that
Tess names her Mouse. When Tess moves to the Portland area, Mouse comes with
her. For the next ten years, apartments, jobs, and routines change, but Mouse
remains the small familiar body who will curl into a travel bag or balance on
Tess's shoulder and go anywhere with her.

Lena Varga belongs to the ordinary part of Tess's Pacific Northwest life.
They are friends, play in the same pub-trivia league, and know enough about one
another's work to recognize when a problem has crossed a disciplinary line.
One night Lena describes a chain of coincidences accumulating around a garage
experiment run by a controls engineer she knows.

Tess asks what was specified before the events occurred. Lena tells her. Tess
asks what records were kept, which cases were excluded, and when the team
decided the chain had begun. By the time the next trivia question arrives,
Lena has stopped trying to summarize.

Tess asks for the raw logs, the complete run history, the precommitted
protocol, and the ordinary instrument records. She expects to find a hidden
dependency, a flexible endpoint, or an innocent decision made after the fact.
The material does not prove Lena's claim, but it survives enough of Tess's
first checks that dismissing it would be its own kind of carelessness.

She agrees to visit the garage.

Evan Hale looks, on first inspection, like a crazy person with unusually good
instrumentation. The garage is crowded with the remains of an ordinary
engineering project: an air liquefier, an LN2-cooled flux pump, a defective
YBCO ring, control electronics, thermal and RF drive hardware, sensors, notes,
and more improvised infrastructure than Tess would allow near a client model.
The records are much better than the room.

Ruth Hale sits in the middle of it. She is Evan's mother, a retired
mathematician, and still part of the work even though dementia repeatedly takes
the recent thread away from her. Mouse settles at the corner of the workbench
and watches everyone.

Tess takes the external coincidence chain apart in front of Evan and Lena. A
dependency they treated as independent runs through the records, and several
choices they believe preceded the result were made only after part of it was
known. The chain that brought Tess to the garage is ordinary after all.

Evan resists the explanation. He checks each link against the original logs,
argues about the boundary cases, and tries to find a place where Tess has
smuggled the answer in. She keeps walking him through it. When the explanation
holds, his skepticism gives way so quickly that Tess mistakes the change for a
new objection.

He is delighted.

The first precommitted physical result inside the apparatus remains unexplained.
Losing the external chain has removed a bad claim and given them a chance to
design a clean one. Evan would rather have the correction than the story he
started with. Tess revises her first impression of him, though not by much.

While Tess drafts the replacement control, Ruth asks one plain mathematical
question. Tess begins to answer, then stops. Ruth is right. Tess rewrites the
control and brings it back to her before the run.

Later, after a long session, Ruth loses the thread and goes into the garden.
Tess follows because she wants air. Ruth points to a volunteer plant and asks
what it is. Tess gives the wrong answer. Ruth teases her, and for the first time
they continue talking without the experiment supplying the subject.

The revised control uses independent inputs, a condition fixed in advance,
and a result that will determine what the apparatus does next. Before the
cutoff, a new chain of ordinary events satisfies that condition. Tess searches
for the shared source and the omitted case. She reconstructs every choice that
could have been made after the result began to emerge.

This time, the explanation does not collapse.

No single event in the chain is impossible. The conjunction is fantastically
improbable. Tess has spent her career explaining how ordinary links make
catastrophes, and she knows the difference between a long causal sequence and
a story assembled backward from an outcome. The garage has produced the first
external anomaly she cannot explain away.

Tess joins the project. She audits categories, questions stopping rules, and
keeps Evan and Lena from turning every remainder into a result. Evan argues
with her constantly and listens closely enough to make the arguments worth
having.

Ruth's first lucid interval occurs during a live task she is already performing
for the project. Her voice steadies. She holds the thread of the conversation,
recognizes the people around her, and finishes work she could not have followed
minutes earlier. Ruth notices the change and asks for another exposure.

The next runs are reduced and monitored. Each interval restores more
continuity and lasts longer before it fades. Tess supports them because Ruth
understands enough to ask and because the improvement is real. During the
clear periods, Ruth returns to the work and to conversations that have learned
to move around her absence.

Outside those intervals, Tess becomes her friend. They sit in the garden. Ruth
can be funny, irritated, confused, and exact within the same hour. Tess waits
when a word or connection disappears and follows the next one Ruth finds.

Mouse chooses Ruth as her preferred resting companion. Some days Ruth greets
her immediately. On other days she studies the little cat as if the visit is
new. Mouse climbs into her lap either way. When she wants Ruth's attention,
she looks up and meows once.

Long garage sessions become nights in the spare room. Every morning Tess packs
Mouse's food, litter supplies, and other necessities to take home. Ruth finally
asks why she keeps doing that and tells her to leave them. Tess does. She keeps
her apartment for a while, but more of her mornings begin in Ruth's kitchen,
and more of her evenings end with Mouse asleep somewhere in the house.

There is no moving day. At some point, home is the place where Ruth knows how
Tess takes her coffee, Mouse has a regular circuit from the garage to the
garden and Ruth's chair, and Evan can find Tess's field guides mixed among his
manuals.

The long arguments with Evan change too. During one dispute over a control,
Tess realizes she has continued well past the point where either of them needs
another example. She is still arguing because she likes the way his mind moves
when it meets resistance and because he likes that hers does not yield.

She kisses him before either of them concedes.

The technical question remains unsettled. They return to it later. By then,
the kiss has changed everything else.

Tess tells Evan about the ferry after their relationship has begun. Her age at
the time and the broad shape of the disaster connect her to a childhood news
story he remembers seeing when he was thirteen. He says so immediately. Tess
tells him what the coverage omitted, including the way its miracle framing
reduced her family to the background of her survival.

Evan calls the event a statistical catastrophe with one living remainder. If
it were a miracle, he says, the deaths would have purchased something. Whatever
meaning Tess's life has belongs to what she does after surviving.

Tess has spent thirty years rejecting people who insist that her survival must
have a purpose. Evan is the first person who gives her a way to speak about
meaning without sending it backward to justify the dead.

They become lovers. Tess keeps her apartment a little longer, mostly as an
administrative fact. Mouse, Ruth, Evan, and the work have already made the
house the center of her ordinary life.

During Ruth's clearest interval yet, she asks after a granddaughter whom she
remembers as part of the family. Evan remembers the pregnancy ending in
miscarriage. Ruth's chronology is coherent, and the emotion attached to the
young woman is specific. Nothing about the memory feels loose or newly
invented to Ruth.

Lena refuses to call the interval a restoration. Evan calls it an overshoot
and proposes a concordance window: the strongest lucidity at which Ruth's
autobiography still agrees with the surviving records. Tess hears the hidden
standard immediately. The proposed measurement cannot distinguish Ruth being
wrong from the records being incomplete, and it makes Evan's history the line
beyond which Ruth is no longer allowed to count as herself.

Before the argument can be resolved, Tess undergoes her first direct contact.
Repeated runs have already changed the conditions around everyone in the
garage. During one of the later intervals, ordinary sound stops arriving from
ordinary places.

The fan, a relay, a human breath, and the small movement of Mouse on a surface
remain distinct. Their intervals begin to carry distance. Pitch and timing
place Tess among them more precisely than sight does, then continue beyond any
room those sources could occupy. Near and far do not reverse. They enter a
relation for which reversal is too simple.

Something turns through that relation.

Tess does not see an object rotating outside herself. She hears adjacency
change while every local sound remains continuous. The turn carries depth,
orientation, and her own position through a direction her body cannot face.
When she tries to locate herself apart from it, that act becomes another
interval inside the same structure.

The relation changes again and remains recognizably itself. Sound, geometry,
meaning, and bodily orientation exchange roles without becoming a message.
Tess waits for it to resolve into something she can answer. It does not. The
contact persists beyond anything she can understand.

Then the garage becomes a room again. Sound returns to sources. Tess can point
to the fan, the relay, Mouse, and the person speaking to her. She cannot put
the impossible turn into an ordinary diagram, but she can describe which
relations stayed fixed while everything used to represent them changed.

Evan's earlier accounts are no longer dismissible as private afterimages of
his altered states. Tess has encountered something real. She still has no
theory of what it is.

Later, their notes give the recurring relation the working name Turning Knot.

Around the same period, people's responses to the project grow easier to
anticipate. Tess's do not. Evan begins asking her more particular questions
about small decisions and sometimes writes down her answers afterward. She
notices. Evan logs nearly everything, her judgments change which tests run,
and they are newly intimate. The attention reads as work mixed with affection,
which is what much of their life has become.

Lena takes the original defective ring to her shared facility. Her group
measures its asymmetric material structure and operating conditions with
independent instruments, then returns it to Evan. They fabricate a new ring
with the same relevant structure and build their own control implementation.
The analogue produces the effect with neither Evan nor Tess present.

Tess is relieved by what the success rules out and unsettled by the success
itself. Whatever the basic apparatus is doing, it does not require her unusual
history or Evan's mind. The result cannot tell them whether their earlier
participation has already shaped the particular chain spreading outward from
the garage.

Lena organizes a small closed replication study. Each site makes its own
analogue and runs it with local equipment, local operators, and agreed
comparison conditions. Several reproduce the operator effect and the bias
toward live consequences.

The institutions around the study see more than a scientific anomaly. A
device that can make an otherwise uncertain outcome arrive is a source of
wealth and power. Every group has a practical goal that makes one more run
sound reasonable, especially when the danger still belongs to someone else's
model.

Tess follows the chains between sites. A failed run in one place releases a
resource another site needs before a fixed cutoff. Procurement, weather,
staffing, and local decisions complete the handoff. Each step has an ordinary
cause. The full conjunction keeps landing on the decisions that permit another
projector run.

> **Unresolved institutional-middle stub:** The particular laboratories, their
> physical projects and ambitions, the practical boundary called containment,
> the first cross-laboratory consequence chain, and the ordinary mechanics of
> the fatal operation remain unresolved. The eventual sequence must preserve
> the accepted human result: one researcher dies, and a close collaborator
> survives with technical competence intact but no autobiographical memory of
> the dead person or their years together. Physical injury initially leaves the
> selective loss medically ambiguous. The accident may remain local or may
> involve causal overlap between lineage-linked operations. It does not prove
> the first independent carrier.

The death stops every site. Equipment is powered down and placed in a safe
state. Data, controller states, and physical evidence are preserved. The
survivor can read joint records and accept that the dead researcher was a close
collaborator, but the shared life exists only as information supplied by other
people.

Tess cannot yet show that the projector removed those years. Trauma remains a
plausible cause. She can show that the safeguards failed and that nobody knows
where the causal boundary of a run now ends.

The evidence hold becomes a formal containment program. A few sites eventually
restart under shared exposure limits, common approval, hard trips, and
coordinated decisions about isolation or decommissioning. Other replicas stay
off. Evan powers down the garage apparatus and joins the selected-site restart
as a technical adviser. Tess's audits shape the stop rules and decide which
anomalies receive another run.

Lena undergoes direct contact and returns convinced that the containment
system may no longer stand outside the thing it measures. Measurements,
reviews, approvals, and shutdown responses can form the same sort of live loop
as the hardware. Proving that suspicion would require a test whose result
changes what the sites do next.

Lena refuses to take part.

Evan argues that suspicion will not keep several laboratories and institutions
shut down. Each site controls its own equipment, and each has reasons to
believe one more bounded run is worth the risk. He brings the carrier
hypothesis, the recursive danger, and Lena's refusal to the people with
authority over the study. They approve a common test.

The initial measurements fail to show an independent carrier. Under the
approved protocol, that result triggers the promised containment and
decommissioning actions. Each site follows the plan in front of it.

Tess watches those actions change conditions elsewhere. One response changes
what another site can observe or must decide. The sequence propagates until it
returns to the conditions for another live consequential run. Only then does
the global pattern become visible.

The protocol has completed the cycle it was designed to detect.

The operation no longer needs Evan or the garage apparatus. Lena ends her
active collaboration. Evan turns immediately toward coordinated stopping work.
Tess has heard Lena refuse the live response while one uncommitted choice still
remained. Now she hears Evan call the proof necessary and begin designing the
next response.

After a later distributed run, Tess remembers a distinction from her own life
that nobody else remembers. It once existed in ordinary messages, records, and
other people's accounts. Now the records agree with one another and exclude it.
The people she asks do the same.

Tess checks every source she can reach. There is no clerical break, no isolated
database loss, and no person who remembers enough to confirm her account. For
the first time since the hospital, she knows something central happened and
has no surviving external chain with which to prove it.

During shared contact, the missing distinction is present again.

Tess enters through sound and spatial relation. The Turning Knot's impossible
turn is recognizably the one she encountered alone, but now the relation holds
structured remnants of the erased fact. Adjacencies persist where ordinary
records contain gaps. Details Tess never told Evan occupy fixed positions in
the acoustic structure.

Evan experiences a bounded visual form. When they return, his account contains
the same impossible turn and the same missing relations. He knows details he
could not have learned from Tess. Their descriptions do not resemble one
another on the surface, but each preserves the same order.

A later distributed run removes part of the residue. Tess hears fewer possible
relations. Evan's visual account loses the corresponding structure. The
matching loss rules out a problem of access. The operation is making histories
inaccessible and then stripping degrees of freedom from what contact still
preserves.

Ruth's granddaughter returns to Tess's mind with a different weight. The
paired evidence places that memory inside an inhabited history the operation
was taking away. Evan's concordance window would have called the surviving
record healthy and Ruth's memory defective.

Within the shared contact, Evan's voice breaks off. A relay clicks in the
pause. Tess turns toward it, and the scrape of her shoe becomes the interval
that brings the voice back. She stops moving. Her next breath supplies the
interval instead. Each interruption becomes the timing of the next repetition.
The relation closes through every difference she introduces before she can
decide what to do with it.

When Evan later describes the same structure as guidance, Tess recognizes it
as a reply made on her behalf. She can still tell the reply is not hers.

Evan admits what he has done before Tess discovers it herself. He tells her
that he began treating her choices as a separate signal after he suspected her
rare survival might be affecting the operation. He kept her in live garage
runs, compared her ordinary decisions with what happened around the apparatus,
and searched the records of the ferry, rescue, and medical treatment to
estimate how unlikely this version of her was.

"What exactly did you use?" Tess asks.

Evan starts with the official rescue timeline. He begins reciting the failed
approaches, the temporary path that let rescuers reach her, her extraction,
and the attempts to reach the rest of her family.

"I know the timeline," Tess says. "Tell me what you did with it."

He tells her that he turned the rescue sequence into a model of the bottleneck
that left her alive when the others were not. He combined it with the medical
record of her hypothermia and resuscitation, built a rarity estimate, and
compared it with ordinary choices he logged during garage runs.

"Show me," Tess says.

Evan opens the complete folder on the garage workstation. Tess reviews the
source records, the model, the calculations, the timestamped choice logs, and
the notes tying them to particular observations and runs. The work stayed in
the garage. None of it entered the consortium test or went to Lena or the
other laboratories.

Evan remains beside her and answers every question. Tess sees enough to
understand the scope. She also sees moments she remembers as ordinary pieces
of their life reduced to inputs in a private analysis she did not know existed.

Timestamps from ordinary conversations line up with columns Tess never knew he
was filling. She remembers each particular question from the other side of the
screen.

Evan's model describes histories containing the adult Tess as exceptionally
sparse. Most nearby chains end with her death, neurological injury, a different
guardian, or a different adult life. The operation can replace many people
with nearby versions who make the response it requires. Tess offers almost no
such substitute. It can preserve her substantially intact or remove her.

The independent analogue has already shown that the basic effect does not need
Tess. The files cannot answer the worse question now in front of her: whether
her choices helped the garage lineage become the carrier that now runs without
either of them.

Tess asks Evan when he first suspected it. The timestamps place his suspicion
before the replica accident, before the proof-to-stop test, and before the
carrier closed a cycle without the garage. She was still walking into live
runs, choosing controls, and helping decide what the project would do next. She
could have stopped. She could have left. Evan did not tell her until leaving
could no longer undo any part she might already have played.

Neither of them can tell Tess whether she helped the anomaly get out of control.
That is what frightens her. If she helped turn the anomaly into a carrier, she
kept participating after Evan suspected it. He let her continue without knowing
the possibility existed.

Evan tells her that asking permission would have changed the signal. He says
their meeting may have been inevitable within the history that survived.

Tess tells him that asking would have changed the signal because it might have
changed her participation. That was the choice he withheld. The claim about
their meeting answers nothing. She still loves him. She no longer trusts that
he will tell her when an uncertainty also belongs to her.

Ruth is already declining outside the lucid intervals. She tires easily,
needs more help moving, and has trouble eating and swallowing. Tess stays with
her. Evan dismantles the active core of the original apparatus and takes it to
a remote test site, believing distance will keep the physical danger away from
the house while he attempts a coordinated shutdown.

Tess and Mouse remain with Ruth. Before Evan leaves, he kisses Ruth on the top
of the head, pets Mouse, and says, "Love you, I'll be back."

The question remains unfinished. Tess is frightened by what her choices may
already have helped set loose, and afraid for Evan as he carries the machine
away. She stays with Ruth.

At the remote site, Evan begins warming the YBCO ring through its
superconducting transition and opening the local feedback loop while the
participating laboratories start their own decommissioning steps. The safety
state of the garage and house is one declared input to the shared response.

At home, light fills the windows.

The sky darkens behind it. Glowing points fall through the black like molten
glass or fire. The ground moves, the garage disappears into a collapsing
section of the neighborhood, and the shockwave strikes the house.

Tess reaches for her phone. There is no service. The roads, utilities, and
emergency access fail with the communications. The house remains standing.
The garage is gone. Mouse appears from the confusion without an injury.

The lost safety signal changes what the participating sites do next. They halt
the shutdown and enter the fallback run promised by the protocol. By the time
Tess can know any of this, the response has already closed another cycle.

Ruth becomes lucid without the apparatus.

For a while, she knows Tess, follows the conversation, and speaks with the
clarity the project taught them to hope for. Tess lets the interval belong to
Ruth. She puts no instrument between them, and there is nobody left to call.
She stays beside Ruth as the interval gives way to exhaustion.

Ruth has increasing trouble speaking and swallowing. Her responses become
intermittent, then stop. Tess remains with her through unconsciousness and
death. Mouse settles close, looks up, and meows once. Ruth does not answer.

Evan returns to find Ruth dead, Tess alive, and Mouse unharmed. The garage is a
ruin, the complete original rig is still powered down at the remote site, and
the failed shutdown offers him another system to inspect.

He does not go to the crater. He does not return to the rig. He stays with
Tess.

Their argument remains unfinished. The folder in the garage is gone without
answering whether Tess helped shape the carrier or restoring the chance to
withdraw that Evan denied her. His return does not settle either question. He
sits beside her, leaves the remote rig and its answers alone, and grieves Ruth
with her.

They sort what remains of Ruth's life and answer questions from people who do
not know enough of the story to ask the right ones. When they can, they bury
her. Evan keeps choosing the person in front of him over the unfinished system
waiting at the remote site. Tess begins to trust the repetition of that choice
more than she could have trusted one apology.

The operation continues without them.

Several weeks of increasingly brittle coincidences follow. One night, millions
of people speak the same sentence at the same instant. Later, satellites in
separate orbits receive valid collision warnings. Operators and autonomous
systems make locally defensible avoidance maneuvers, and those maneuvers drive
the satellites into one another. The sky fills with flashes and falling
debris.

Communications fail. Roads close. Each local response still has a reason, but
the complete histories converge on fewer and more destructive outcomes.

Before severing her outbound links, Lena told Tess she was withdrawing to a
remote university geomagnetic observatory. Tess decides to find her friend.
Evan goes with her. Mouse travels in the same bag and on the same shoulder that
have carried her since Alaska.

They reach the observatory after the satellite collisions and before the next
planetary failure. Lena recognizes them and remembers why she came. She can
explain what the passive instruments have recorded. Yet different new
questions begin producing the same reply. Tess changes the wording, waits, and
asks about something else. Lena returns to the same sentence and the same
small motion while the rest of her knowledge remains intact.

While Lena can still make one different choice, she refuses to let Evan test
the change. Evan turns away from the instruments, sits beside her, and stays.
Tess recognizes the same choice he has kept making since he returned to the
house.

Continental margins rupture in a synchronized sequence. Signals reach the
observatory from different parts of the planet. Tess hears the event in the
building before she can take in the plots: long-period vibrations pass through
the floor and walls, separate arrivals combine, and the whole station answers
like one struck body.

Lena remains physically present, but the next distinct response never comes.

Stable reality fails soon afterward.

Tess and Evan each follow an ordinary route through the same damaged
landmarks. Tess keeps her position through footsteps, voices, echoes, surfaces,
and operating machinery. Evan follows bounded objects and visible passages.
When they compare where they went, both routes are locally complete, but they
cannot belong to one map.

A physical trace crosses both routes. The contradiction is outside them.

The failure spreads. Distant replica sites become physically co-present with
the observatory. Rooms overlap without sharing one geometry. Objects and
signals still cross between Tess's acoustic relations and Evan's visible
spaces, but place, observer, and contact no longer separate cleanly.

The remote site where Evan left the powered-down apparatus becomes accessible
inside the damaged observatory. Relay clicks, fans, and the sounds of the old
rig enter Tess's local atlas from a place that cannot fit the route by which
they arrived.

In the collapsing relation around them, Tess again feels a reply begin before
her own response is complete. Every objection becomes the condition for the
next movement. Every available stop returns as another continuation. She can
still locate the small difference between the response supplied for her and
the one she has not yet made.

Evan begins working out one last use of the rig. Tess follows the proposed
shutdown forward and finds it closing back through him. If Evan can specify
every outcome, observe every input, and decide what happens next, then the act
still has only one author. The operation can use his response as another step.

The ending requires a second choice that remains outside Evan's control.

Tess identifies the requirement. Evan works out how the powered-down apparatus
can receive both choices. They tell each other everything they know about what
the act can do, what it will cost, and what may happen if their answers differ.
Once the act begins, neither will be able to inspect the other's decision.

Either of them can refuse.

Mouse crosses one of the worst overlap regions by following the surface under
her paws, familiar voices, and whatever scent remains continuous. She settles
somewhere Evan can identify as a bounded place and Tess can locate through the
sound of her claws.

For one moment, Mouse is a fact they still share.

Then Tess and Evan pass beyond one another's ability to verify what happens
next.

Tess knows the cost of choosing. She also knows the cost of a mismatch. If the
operation fails and everyone beside her disappears, she may again become the
one living remainder around whom other people build a finished story.

There is no surviving record to consult, no control to improve, and no further
question that can reveal Evan's answer before Tess gives her own. Her survival
did not happen for a reason. She can still decide what to do with it.

Tess makes her choice without knowing his.

Their choices meet.

The viewpoint passes to Mouse. She hears Tess and Evan speak their final words
from places that no longer fit together. She watches both familiar bodies turn
toward something arriving through their different spaces. Light reaches
everything at once.
