# Ruth Hale: full character synopsis

Status: accepted
Snowflake step: 05

This treatment alternates between close and external third in the present
tense. It follows Ruth through the changing boundary between collaboration and
care. This is the accepted full treatment.

## Synopsis

Near the end of her working life, Ruth finds a page of mathematics in her own
handwriting and has no memory of writing it. The work is correct. This annoys
her more than it frightens her.

She starts dating every page in larger writing. Soon the dates tell her when
something happened without making it feel like something she did. A familiar
proof may open cleanly in front of her while the name of the person who brought
it disappears. She can still hear a bad assumption from across a room. She
just can't always remember entering the room.

For a while, Ruth's oldest habits cover the breaks. She has spent decades
teaching and doing mathematics, and much of that structure remains available
without introduction. She knows when two terms have been made to look alike by
notation. She knows when somebody has hidden an answer inside a premise. She
can lose the last ten minutes and still know the argument is wrong.

The breaks widen. Ruth leaves formal work. When it becomes clear that she
can't safely live alone, Evan begins a careful discussion about options. Ruth
ends it by telling him which room he can have. He moves into her house.

He brings clothes, his work computer, and far more equipment than any one
person should own without a loading dock. The garage absorbs most of it. Ruth
keeps the kitchen and garden arranged the way she knows them, although labels,
pill organizers, and a second set of keys begin appearing around the house.

Evan takes over the driving and keeps track of appointments. On bad days Ruth
knows he is familiar before she can place him as her son. On better ones she
remembers enough to resent being reminded of something she has already done.
The better and worse days refuse to line up in a useful order.

The garage gives them somewhere to meet as adults. Evan is building an
LN2-cooled flux pump, hoping to make a persistent field source for portable
imaging. Ruth reads the experiment through its conditions and run classes. She
checks the parts she can hold in one sitting and asks what each result would
actually prove.

Evan checks her work again after she leaves or falls asleep on the garage
couch. His second set of marks is easy to find. Once Ruth lays his page beside
hers and asks whether the paper enjoyed doing the same work twice. Evan laughs,
then keeps checking.

Sometimes he finds a mistake. Sometimes he gets her answer. Ruth can live with
verification. What she watches is whether the second pass quietly becomes the
only pass that counts.

The first impossible result appears in data Ruth is reviewing. The old YBCO
ring falls short of the trapped field they need, and a diode monitoring the
traveling heat pulse reports a cold notch below the cold-stage temperature.
The neighboring sensors warm normally. Ruth circles the notch and asks which
reference failed.

Evan replaces the probe and the path through the data logger. The notch comes
back in the same place on the ring. Ruth circles it again.

Later she opens a log and finds the same anomaly already marked in her own
handwriting. She knows the mark is hers. She cannot recover making it. The
machine is repeating itself more reliably than she is, but the trace is still
wrong. She points it out to Evan.

The expensive replacement ring arrives and meets the field target without the
notch. Evan and Ruth celebrate. He gives her the good news again over dinner,
and she is pleased a second time.

The next time Ruth enters the garage, the old ring is cold and running. She
doesn't remember seeing Evan install it, but she knows him well enough to
understand why it is there. A solved problem has never interested him as much
as one that refuses to stay solved.

After that, the work begins moving faster than Ruth can. Lena Varga starts
coming to the house to check Evan's claims. The garage acquires new instruments,
new phrases, and conversations that continue after Ruth goes inside. Ruth can
no longer recover what every run is meant to decide before the work moves to
the next one.

At first, Evan waits and catches her up. Then he catches her up after beginning
without her. Eventually Ruth returns to questions that have already been
answered and decisions that have already become hardware.

She doesn't see a moment when their project becomes his. It happens in small
amounts, each one explained by the state of the ring, a useful window in the
data, or the fact that Ruth was asleep. The two chairs remain beside the
workbench. Her notes remain in the binder. The work stops treating her absence
as a reason to pause.

Tess Kertesz arrives to audit one of the claims that appears to have escaped
the garage. She brings a stack of records and a tiny black-and-brown cat named
Mouse. Mouse steps out of a bag, inspects the people and equipment, and takes
the corner of the workbench as if it has been left open for her.

Tess spends her first day taking the claim apart in front of Evan and Lena.
Evan argues until he is satisfied, then becomes so pleased by the correction
that Ruth laughs at him. He would rather have his favorite result taken away
properly than keep it through bad work.

While Tess rebuilds the test, Ruth asks whether two conditions called
independent have already been joined earlier in the protocol. Tess stops,
checks, and changes the design. She brings it back to Ruth before the run.

During another argument, Evan tries to collapse two alternatives because both
lead to the outcome he wants. Ruth reaches for the classroom language that
still comes without effort. Unlike terms do not cancel because the answer would
be tidier. Independent alternatives remain independent until the work joins
them. Tess carries the distinction away with her.

After the long session, Ruth loses the thread and goes to the garden. Tess
follows for air. Ruth asks her to identify a volunteer plant. Tess gets it
wrong. Ruth teases her, and they continue talking without the experiment.

Tess returns the next day. Then she returns often enough that Ruth no longer
needs to ask why she is there.

One evening Ruth falls asleep on the garage couch while the defective ring is
approaching a narrow usable state. Evan begins a mapping-reversal confirmation
without waking her. When Ruth opens her eyes, the fans are at run speed, the
live-state indicators are lit, and the check that belonged to her is already
closed.

The missing interval doesn't matter yet. The machine is running. Tess is
watching a trace, Evan is calling the next classification, and Ruth can see
that nobody expected her participation before the decision became
irreversible.

She stands and takes the part of the work that remains.

As Ruth begins classifying the live response, the effort of holding one thing
while reaching for the next disappears. The plot arrives as a whole relation.
The apparatus state, Evan's question, and the answer occupy one piece of
thought. Ruth knows the feeling. It is the absorbed ease of following a proof
far enough that each line contains the need for the next one.

Then the room comes with it.

The relay belongs to the sound it makes. The coffee on the bench belongs to
the hand that set it there. Tess is Tess. Evan is her son. Ruth knows what they
are doing, what happened before she woke, and where she stands inside it.

She finishes the classification. The run ends safely, but the clarity remains
long enough for everyone to notice. Evan begins asking questions. Ruth answers
the ones about the work, then asks him why he completed her check without her.

He tells her the ring state would not have held. Ruth tells him that doing her
part is different from doing it with her.

Evan apologizes. He is embarrassed and sincere. He promises to wait next time.

Ruth asks for another exposure.

She knows what the interval gave her because she was there to receive it. For
a little while, people stayed connected to their names. A conversation kept
its beginning. She could finish work without finding herself halfway through a
problem somebody else had started for her. When the state faded, she retained
enough of the relief to want it again.

The next runs are smaller and watched closely. Ruth takes part in the decision
each time. The intervals recur across the following months, sometimes longer,
sometimes shorter, and each becomes part of life in the house rather than a
single miracle everyone keeps trying to recreate.

During one, Ruth carries an argument from the garage into dinner and still
knows what they were discussing when the plates arrive. During another, she
returns to a half-finished relation in her notes and remembers why she began
it. Once she spends most of the recovered time listening to Tess describe a
storm model that has nothing to do with the ring.

The clear periods do not end at a clean boundary. A graph mark remains after
the argument around it loosens. Soil stays cold under Ruth's fingernails after
she forgets why she went outside. Mouse's weight remains against her leg when
she can no longer recover whose cat she is.

Mouse chooses Ruth as her preferred resting companion. She climbs into Ruth's
lap without waiting to be remembered. When she wants attention, she looks up
and meows once. Ruth usually answers with a word or a hand along her back. If
Ruth studies her as a new animal, Mouse waits and asks again.

Tess begins sleeping in the spare room after late runs. During one of those
nights, Ruth notices Tess watching Evan after he turns back to the controls.
The look has nothing to do with the machine. A little later, Ruth catches Evan
glancing at Tess while Tess is bent over a graph. It is the same look, hidden
just as badly.

Ruth is filled with a secret joy and says nothing.

Every morning Tess packs Mouse's food and litter supplies to take home. Ruth
watches this happen until she understands that Tess still thinks she is
visiting. Ruth's invitation isn't exactly sly, but it isn't exactly not either.
She asks why the cat's things keep leaving a house they return to each night
and tells Tess to leave them.

There is no moving day. The bags stop traveling. Mouse develops a circuit
through the garage, kitchen, garden, and Ruth's chair. Tess's field guides
appear among Evan's manuals. More often, Ruth wakes to find Tess already in the
house.

Tess knows how to help without making Ruth perform her confusion. She sets out
medication and water, then turns to something nearby. One afternoon Ruth stops
in a doorway with no idea what she meant to do next. Tess says she is going to
the garden and leaves room for Ruth to come with her.

Once Tess reaches for Ruth too soon, and Ruth knocks her hand away. Tess backs
off. The next time, she waits.

On days when Ruth cannot place the whole situation, she can still read the
patience in Tess's posture and voice.

The looks are followed by arguments that continue after the technical point has
run out of work to do. Tess and Evan stand closer. Later they stop using the
spare room in the same way. Ruth sees a household gathering around her that she
quietly helped make room for.

Meanwhile, her body is giving up ground the projector never returns. Garage
sessions leave her tired sooner. Stairs take longer. She needs help standing,
then resents the help if it arrives before she asks. Meals get smaller. Tess
quietly changes what they eat and sits beside her with the same food.

Sometimes the mind the apparatus returns is clear enough to understand exactly
how weak the body has become. Ruth can hold the room and still need Tess's arm
to cross it. She can finish a mathematical thought and fail to swallow the
next bite. The two kinds of loss proceed on their own schedules.

During Ruth's clearest interval yet, the present opens far enough to include a
continuous life behind it. She follows the work, remembers the house, and knows
the people around her without effort.

She asks after her granddaughter.

The young woman is not a new image or a loose name. She has a place in the
family and a history that joins cleanly to Ruth's own. Ruth asks about her with
the mild expectation of somebody continuing an ordinary conversation.

Evan goes still. He asks who she means. When Ruth explains, he tells her that
the pregnancy ended in miscarriage.

Ruth searches the life available to her and finds no empty place where he says
there should be one. At the same time, she can see that Evan is not humoring
her. His grief is immediate and old. He remembers a child who never lived.
Ruth remembers the woman that child became.

The interval fades before either history can make room for the other.

Evan calls the result an overshoot. He draws a line on the whiteboard and names
it the concordance window: the strongest clarity at which Ruth's life still
agrees with the records he trusts. Lena objects. Tess objects. The argument
continues after Ruth can no longer hold all of it.

The next time Ruth can follow the whiteboard, the same flaw is waiting. Evan's
records have become the answer against which Ruth is being measured. Anything
in her life that disagrees with him will count against the run, no matter how
coherent the rest of her is.

Before the disagreement is settled, Evan runs another part of the apparatus
while Ruth is resting.

Ruth comes back to a safe machine and a changed room. Tess is unhurt, but she
listens to the garage as if ordinary sounds have acquired positions the rest of
them cannot hear. The controller state shows that the work advanced while Ruth
was away.

Ruth cannot reconstruct enough of the recent sequence to establish exactly
what Evan decided. She knows her son. She knows what happens when work offers
him a reason to keep moving. That is enough.

She lets it go.

There are arguments worth spending the available hour on. Ruth chooses the one
about the concordance window. She still wants another lucid interval, but
agreement with Evan's history comes out of the target. He does not get to
define her by selecting the version of her that agrees with him.

Lena remains worried. Ruth listens, asks questions, and requests one final
reduced exposure under the narrower terms. The team agrees.

The run brings the room back. The granddaughter remains in the same place in
Ruth's life. After the required safety checks, Evan turns to the longer page of
questions he has prepared. Ruth places her hand over it.

He has enough.

Ruth leaves the garage with Tess and Mouse. They spend the remaining interval
between the garden and kitchen. Nothing they do needs a score. Ruth talks when
she wants to, Tess lets silence stand, and Mouse settles wherever Ruth sits.

The clarity begins to loosen while Ruth has soil on her hands and Mouse under
one of them. She loses the start of a sentence. Tess waits. Ruth finds another
one. When that thread goes too, they remain where they are.

Nobody knows it is Ruth's last projector-linked lucid interval.

Afterward, the project becomes something that mostly happens elsewhere. Other
groups copy the ring and build their own machines. The garage fills with faces
on screens and the vocabulary of institutions: sites, approvals, holds,
containment, restart. Ruth knows some of the words and loses the attachments
between them.

The ambitions beyond the house reach her as surnames Evan repeats, calls he
takes through meals, and another promised hour that becomes an afternoon.

Then the garage goes dark. Evan sits at the kitchen table instead of the
workbench. When Ruth asks why, he tells her that one of the other runs killed a
researcher. A close collaborator survived with the technical knowledge needed
to read their shared work and no autobiographical memory of the person who
died.

The next day Ruth asks why the garage is dark, and Evan tells her again. On one
telling she follows enough to understand that the work survived inside
somebody after the life around it was gone.

Ruth asks for the dead researcher's name. Evan gives it to her. She writes it
down.

The note remains after the name leaves.

The machines eventually restart under rules meant to keep them contained.
Evan advises the groups from Ruth's house. He is home more often and available
less. The garage light returns at hours when Ruth is awake and when she is not.

Her own world contracts. Fatigue becomes sleep. Standing becomes a slow
negotiation with the chair. Speech takes enough effort that she sometimes
abandons a sentence whose meaning she still knows. Swallowing becomes a task
that can consume a meal.

Tess adapts with her. She makes the food easier, eats beside her, and leaves
Ruth enough privacy to refuse. When Ruth stalls between rooms, Tess says where
she is going and gives her a path to join. Mouse moves from Ruth's lap to the
space against her side, then to the hollow between her arm and the chair.

Eventually Evan tells Ruth that the work can continue without the machine in
their garage. He calls the larger operation a carrier. The word remains after
the explanation loses its attachments. Lena stops coming. Evan begins planning
a larger shutdown.

Evan also tells Ruth that he no longer believes the granddaughter was a
mistake. He tries to explain that she belonged to a real history that is now
gone. Ruth follows enough to understand that he has changed his answer. Before
he can ask what the news means to her, the explanation loses its beginning. He
tries again on another day.

Around the same time, something changes between Evan and Tess. They spend a
long time in the garage with the door closed. Tess comes back into the house
frightened and very still. When Evan enters, she makes room for him without
looking at him. He starts an explanation and leaves it unfinished.

Ruth cannot keep the details they give her, but the new distance survives each
lapse. Whatever Evan did, the work arrived before the conversation again.

Tess stays with Ruth. She sets out medication, adjusts meals, and sleeps close
enough to hear Ruth try to stand. Her care remains quiet. Her ease with Evan is
gone.

Evan dismantles the active core of the garage machine and prepares to take it
to a remote test site. Distance, he says, will keep the physical danger away
from Ruth and Tess while he tries to stop the larger operation. The defective
ring and the rest of the active core leave the garage in pieces and depart
together.

Before Evan leaves, he kneels beside Ruth's chair. He kisses the top of her
head, runs a hand along Mouse's back, and says, "Love you, I'll be back."

Ruth leans into the kiss and keeps her hand over his for a moment. The promise
passes through the room without finding words from her.

After he leaves, Ruth sleeps while Tess moves between her and the windows.

Light fills the house.

The ground throws objects from shelves. A section of the neighborhood
collapses, and an explosion destroys the garage. The shockwave breaks windows
and kills the power. Smoke and a blackened sky replace the familiar view
outside.

Tess finds Mouse alive and unhurt. Her phone is dead. The roads are blocked,
the utilities are gone, and no emergency response can reach the house. Ruth's
decline continues inside the isolated rooms, with Tess and Mouse beside her.

In the quiet after the blast, Ruth wakes clearly.

She knows Tess. She knows Mouse. She knows Evan has taken the machine away to
work and may not return before she dies. Her mind is clear inside a body that
remains weak. The room returns to her as it is.

Tess sits beside her. Mouse settles against her. Ruth feels each breath ask a
little more than the one before it.

"If I die before he gets back, make sure he knows how much I love him," Ruth
says.

She tells Tess what a lifetime with Evan has taught her. When grief or loss
comes close enough that he cannot fix it, he finds work that lets him stand one
step away. He can spend a night repairing the wrong thing and bring the repair
back as proof that he cared. The work may be useful. The love may be real. The
person who needed him is still alone.

Work is where Evan goes when he is afraid to stay.

When Ruth has said what she can, she closes her eyes. Tess stays.

Fatigue pulls at Ruth's speech. Words become harder to finish. Swallowing fails
more often. Tess gives her water when she can take it and stops when she
cannot.

Ruth can no longer hold the whole room, but Mouse's weight remains against her
side and Tess's hand remains around hers. Her attention narrows to contact,
breath, and the effort of staying awake. Then even those relations pass beyond
what she can follow.

Ruth responds intermittently for a while. She slips into unconsciousness. Tess
and Mouse remain beside her as her breathing becomes shallow, pauses, and
stops.

Mouse looks up at Ruth and meows once for her attention.

Ruth does not answer.
